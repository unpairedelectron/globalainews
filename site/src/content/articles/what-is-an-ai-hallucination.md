## The bottom line

**A hallucination is a plausible but false statement, produced confidently.** OpenAI's definition: "instances where a model confidently generates an answer that isn't true". [1]

The useful part is not the definition but the cause, and it is not mysterious. OpenAI's research paper "argues that language models hallucinate because standard training and evaluation procedures reward guessing over acknowledging uncertainty". [1]

Read that twice. The problem is not only in the model. It is in the scoreboard. A model that says "I don't know" scores zero; a model that guesses sometimes gets lucky. Over thousands of benchmark questions, the guesser looks better.

## Where hallucinations come from

The mechanism is specific, and it explains why models misspell almost nothing but invent dates freely.

Models learn first through pretraining: predicting the next word across huge amounts of text. As OpenAI puts it, "there are no 'true/false' labels attached to each statement. The model sees only positive examples of fluent language and must approximate the overall distribution." [1]

Then comes the analogy that makes it click. Imagine labelling millions of pet photos not as "cat" or "dog" but with **each pet's birthday**. "Since birthdays are essentially random, this task would always produce errors, no matter how advanced the algorithm." [1]

That is the difference between the two kinds of mistake: "Spelling and parentheses follow consistent patterns, so errors there disappear with scale. But arbitrary low-frequency facts, like a pet's birthday, cannot be predicted from patterns alone and hence lead to hallucinations." [1]

So a model is reliable where the training data contains a *pattern* and unreliable where it contains only an *arbitrary fact* seen rarely. Names, dates, citations, phone numbers, section numbers of regulations — all arbitrary, all low-frequency, all exactly where models fail.

The academic literature frames the same problem more broadly. A 2023 survey describes LLMs as "prone to hallucination, generating plausible yet nonfactual content", raising "significant concerns over the reliability of LLMs in real-world information retrieval systems". [2] An earlier survey notes that deep-learning generation "is prone to hallucinate unintended text", across summarisation, dialogue, question answering, data-to-text and machine translation. [3]

## Why it persists: the scoreboard problem

This is the part most explainers miss, and it is the most actionable.

OpenAI's framing is a multiple-choice test. "If you do not know the answer but take a wild guess, you might get lucky and be right. Leaving it blank guarantees a zero. In the same way, when models are graded only on accuracy... they are encouraged to guess rather than say 'I don't know.'" [1]

Put concretely: asked for a birthday it does not know, a model guessing "September 10" has a 1-in-365 chance. "Saying 'I don't know' guarantees zero points." [1]

Then OpenAI published the table that proves the point, from the SimpleQA evaluation in its GPT‑5 system card: [1]

| | gpt-5-thinking-mini | OpenAI o4-mini |
|---|---|---|
| Abstention rate | 52% | 1% |
| Accuracy rate | 22% | 24% |
| **Error rate** | **26%** | **75%** |

The older model wins on accuracy — 24% against 22%. It also gets three quarters of answers *wrong*, against roughly a quarter. As OpenAI concludes: "Strategically guessing when uncertain improves accuracy but increases errors and hallucinations." [1]

If you ever compare two models on a single accuracy number, this table is the reason not to. Most benchmarks "pluck out the accuracy metric, but this entails a false dichotomy between right and wrong". [1] A model that abstains half the time and errs a quarter of the time is far safer for most real work than one that confidently errs three times in four.

## The finding that broke the scaling assumption

Hallucination was measured before it was explained. TruthfulQA, published in 2021, built 817 questions across 38 categories including health, law, finance and politics, deliberately written so "that some humans would answer falsely due to a false belief or misconception". [4]

The results: "The best model was truthful on 58% of questions, while human performance was 94%." [4]

And the part that mattered most: "The largest models were generally the least truthful. This contrasts with other NLP tasks, where performance improves with model size." The authors explain why that is expected rather than paradoxical — "if false answers are learned from the training distribution" — and conclude that "scaling up models alone is less promising for improving truthfulness than fine-tuning using training objectives other than imitation of text from the web". [4]

Those are 2021 figures on 2021 models, and should not be read as current rates. The structural point survives: bigger is not automatically truer.

## Five things people get wrong

OpenAI lists these as misconceptions, with its own findings against each. [1]

1. **"A more accurate model won't hallucinate."** No: "Accuracy will never reach 100% because, regardless of model size, search and reasoning capabilities, some real-world questions are inherently unanswerable."
2. **"Hallucinations are inevitable."** Also no: "They are not, because language models can abstain when uncertain."
3. **"Only big models can avoid them."** The reverse can hold: "It can be *easier* for a small model to know its limits." Being "calibrated" — knowing what you don't know — "requires much less computation than being accurate".
4. **"It's a mysterious glitch."** No: "We understand the statistical mechanisms through which hallucinations arise and are rewarded in evaluations."
5. **"We just need a good hallucination eval."** The hardest one to fix: "a good hallucination eval has little effect against hundreds of traditional accuracy-based evals that penalize humility and reward guessing. Instead, all of the primary eval metrics need to be reworked to reward expressions of uncertainty."

## What actually reduces it

Three things work, in rough order of effort.

**Let the model abstain, and reward it for doing so.** If you build evaluations for your own application, score errors as worse than abstentions. OpenAI's Model Spec takes this line: "it is better to indicate uncertainty or ask for clarification than provide confident information that may be incorrect". [1] A system prompt that permits "I don't know" is a real intervention, not a platitude.

**Put the facts in front of the model instead of hoping they are in the weights.** Arbitrary low-frequency facts are precisely what pretraining cannot learn, so retrieve them at answer time. That is retrieval-augmented generation, and it also gives you a citation to check — see our guide to [fine-tuning versus RAG versus prompting](/article/fine-tuning-vs-rag-vs-prompting/) and our explainer on [context windows, memory and RAG](/article/context-window-vs-memory-vs-rag/). The 2023 survey is candid that this is not a complete fix, discussing "the current limitations faced by retrieval-augmented LLMs in combating hallucinations". [2]

**Check the class of claim, not the whole answer.** Names, dates, numbers, citations and identifiers are the failure modes. Verify those and you catch most of the damage.

## What this means for Indian languages

OpenAI's own illustration of calibration is a language example, and it transfers directly.

"When asked to answer a Māori question, a small model which knows no Māori can simply say 'I don't know' whereas a model that knows some Māori has to determine its confidence." [1]

That middle ground is exactly where Indian languages sit for most frontier models. They are not absent from training data, and they are not richly represented either. A model with partial Hindi, Tamil or Marathi has to judge its own competence — the harder problem — and the arbitrary low-frequency facts of Indian public life are thinner in its training data than their English equivalents.

This compounds a safety point the UN's scientific panel made, covered in our piece on the [Bridgetown Call to Action](/article/un-bridgetown-call-to-action-ai-explained/): models can be less safe, not merely less fluent, outside English. The practical instruction is the same in both cases. Test in the language you serve, and measure the error rate, not just the accuracy rate.

## What we don't know yet

- **Current rates.** OpenAI says its "latest models have lower hallucination rates" but the published comparison here is the GPT‑5-era SimpleQA table. [1]
- **Whether the industry will rework its evals.** The paper's central recommendation is that "all of the primary eval metrics need to be reworked to reward expressions of uncertainty". That is a collective-action problem, not a model fix. [1]
- **How much abstention users will tolerate.** A model that abstains 52% of the time is safer and also less immediately useful. [1]
- **Per-language hallucination rates.** We found no published breakdown for Indian languages.

## FAQ

### What is an AI hallucination?

A plausible but false statement generated confidently by a language model. The term covers invented facts, citations, names and dates, not just obvious nonsense. [1][2]

### Why do models make things up instead of saying they don't know?

Because being graded only on accuracy rewards guessing. A wrong guess sometimes scores; an abstention never does. OpenAI argues this is why hallucination persists even as models improve. [1]

### Does a bigger model hallucinate less?

Not automatically. TruthfulQA found the largest models were generally the least truthful, and OpenAI notes it can be easier for a small model to recognise its own limits, because calibration takes less computation than accuracy. [1][4]

### Can hallucinations be eliminated?

Not by accuracy alone — some questions are "inherently unanswerable". But OpenAI argues they are not inevitable either, because a model can abstain when uncertain. [1]

### Does RAG fix it?

It helps substantially for missing-knowledge failures and gives you a source to check, but it is not a complete fix; the research literature discusses the limits of retrieval-augmented models in combating hallucination. [2]

### Are hallucinations worse in Indian languages?

We found no published per-language rates. But the calibration argument suggests partial coverage is the hardest case, and a model with some Hindi or Tamil must judge its own confidence rather than simply decline. Test in your own language. [1]
