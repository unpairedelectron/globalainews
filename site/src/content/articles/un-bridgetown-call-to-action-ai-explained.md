## The bottom line

**The UN has asked the world to agree an AI risk framework within a year, and admitted in the same breath that it would not be binding.**

At the Bridgetown Special Summit on AI Safety in Barbados on 23 November 2026, co-hosted by the UN and Prime Minister Mia Mottley, Secretary-General António Guterres called on member states "to begin discussions on a multilateral AI Risk Management Framework". His framing: "Put simply, we need a common foundation for AI safety." [1]

The accompanying Bridgetown Call to Action asks member states to develop, within a year, "a globally inclusive, principles-based Multilateral Framework for managing AI risk, with independent and credible oversight", and urges governments, companies, financial institutions and donors to fund AI capacity-building in the Global South, "including early pledges to the proposed Global Fund for AI Capacity-Building". [1]

The substance worth your attention is not the framework. It is the capacity gap underneath it, which the UN quantified — and a finding about language that lands directly on India.

## What the Call to Action actually asks for

Three things, per the UN's own summary. [1]

1. **A framework, within a year.** Member states are asked to develop "a globally inclusive, principles-based Multilateral Framework for managing AI risk, with independent and credible oversight".
2. **Money for capacity.** Governments, the private sector, financial institutions and donors are urged to invest in AI capacity-building in the Global South, with early pledges to the proposed Global Fund.
3. **Signatures.** Barbados and the UN invite countries and stakeholders worldwide to back the Call.

Guterres set out what a common foundation would contain: shared safety measures, common approaches to testing, clear safeguards and standards, basic transparency requirements, independent evaluation and oversight of advanced models, and "support so that every country can put them into practice". [1]

In his prepared remarks he was careful about uniformity: the summit "can help strengthen national approaches, without expecting a single model to fit every country". What he asked for instead was "greater interoperability through comparable evaluations, common terminology and shared learning from serious incidents". [2]

## "Not a treaty": what this would and would not be

The UN's own Special Envoy for Digital and Emerging Technologies, Amandeep Singh Gill, described the proposal in terms that rule out the strongest reading: "It's not a treaty, it's not regulation, but it is a framework which different countries can then use to build up their own national AI safety infrastructure." [3]

That is worth holding onto, because "multilateral framework with independent oversight" reads like an enforcement mechanism and is not being presented as one. It is scaffolding for national capability.

Guterres put the reason for urgency plainly: "The world cannot afford a race to the bottom on AI safety." [2] He also pointed to the Independent International Scientific Panel on AI, whose first report, in his words, "shows that AI guardrails are trailing capabilities". [2]

## The number that explains the whole summit

Gill, citing the World Development Report 2026, gave the gap a figure: **high-income countries have 500 incident response teams; low-income countries have six.** [3]

His point was that the asymmetry is not about who builds models. Many countries lack "not only the computing power and talent to develop AI, but also the capacity to test models developed elsewhere and prepare for incidents such as cybersecurity breaches". [3]

"These AI risks don't recognize borders," he said. Models may be developed in a couple of geographies, "but the risk can travel anywhere and the capacity is not there to deal with those risks". [3]

Against that, the existing infrastructure is modest: the UN-supported Global Network for Exchange and Cooperation on AI Capacity-Building "already has 27 centres of excellence across five regions". [2] Twenty-seven centres and a proposed fund, against a gap of 500 to six.

## The finding that matters most for India

Buried in the coverage is the most actionable item for anyone building or deploying AI in a multilingual country.

The UN-backed Independent International Scientific Panel on AI released a thematic brief on AI capacity-building, which Guterres said "shows how countries are closing gaps in capacity to govern digital technology — in particular evaluation capacity, to ensure systems are safe across languages and cultures". [2]

Panel member Vukosi Marivate of the University of Pretoria put the problem concretely: "AI models still perform far worse and can be less safe across a wide range of tongues, from African languages to Caribbean creoles. Yet over 1,000 of them already have the foundations needed to close that gap." [1]

Read that as a safety claim, not a quality one. It is not only that a model is clumsier in Tamil or Marathi; it is that its guardrails may not hold. A refusal that works in English can fail in another language, and a safety evaluation run only in English will not show it.

India has 22 scheduled languages. The practical implication for an Indian team shipping anything user-facing is that English-only evaluation is not evidence of safety for your users. Our explainer on [why Indian languages cost more to process](/article/what-is-a-token-indian-languages-cost-more/) covers the tokenizer side of the same asymmetry, and our piece on [India's own AI governance consultation](/article/india-ai-consultation-paper-what-is-law/) covers where domestic rules stand.

## Mottley's kill switch

The sharpest practical demand at the summit came from the host. Mottley said small states "often do not have the opportunity to share perspective in the rules that affect them". [1]

Asked whether a frontier AI lab should pay if one of its agents hacked a bank in Barbados, and whether her government should have a "kill switch" to cut off access to a model in an emergency, she answered yes to both: "It has to be a condition of the sale that what I'm buying from you, I'm capable of saying, that's it. No more." [1]

She also warned that "the horse has already bolted from the stables", and that only governments willing to regulate and legislate can rein in the concentration of power. [1]

That concentration was Guterres's theme too. He said power is moving "from States, from governing institutions to a small number of companies and persons", a shift for which post-war institutions including the UN are "today not adequately equipped". [1] He separately warned that the capacity to weaponise AI is "concentrated in two countries and a limited number of companies", and repeated his support for banning autonomous weapons. [1]

## What this means for…

**Indian policymakers and labs.** The evaluation-capacity argument is the one with leverage. India has the languages, the user base and the institutions to run local-context safety testing, and the UN is explicitly asking for it.

**Anyone deploying models in Indian languages.** Treat English-only safety evaluation as incomplete, and test refusals and failure modes in the languages you actually serve.

**Anyone tracking AI governance.** A non-binding framework proposed by an outgoing Secretary-General, asking for agreement within a year, is a weak instrument. The capacity funding is the part that could change outcomes, and it currently exists as a proposal.

## What we don't know yet

- **Whether any state will champion it.** Guterres leaves office at the end of December, so delivery falls to his successor. [1]
- **Whether the Global Fund gets money.** It remains a proposal, with the Call asking only for "early pledges". [1]
- **What the final Call to Action text says.** The un.org pages hosting the summit documents and the panel's thematic brief return 403 to automated requests, so we relied on the UN's own reporting and the Secretary-General's published remarks rather than the source documents. [1][2]
- **The exact count of signatories to related initiatives.** The UN's Friday briefing put the Norway–Finland frontier-model call at 32 states plus the European Commission, and the Paris call at 17 states and 10 AI developers; by Monday, Macron said the Paris text had 18 heads of state and government and 11 AI leaders. [1][3]

## FAQ

### What is the Bridgetown Call to Action?

A call issued by the UN and Barbados at the Bridgetown Special Summit on AI Safety on 23 November 2026, asking member states to develop a multilateral AI risk management framework within a year, with independent oversight, and to fund AI capacity-building in the Global South. [1]

### Is it legally binding?

No. The UN's Special Envoy for Digital and Emerging Technologies described it as "not a treaty, it's not regulation, but… a framework which different countries can then use to build up their own national AI safety infrastructure". [3]

### What is the Global Fund for AI Capacity-Building?

A fund proposed by the Secretary-General to narrow the gap in countries' ability to manage AI risks. The Call asks for early pledges; it is not yet a funded programme. [1][2]

### What did the UN say about AI safety in other languages?

Its Independent International Scientific Panel's thematic brief focuses on evaluation capacity so systems are "safe across languages and cultures". A panel member said models "perform far worse and can be less safe" across many languages, from African languages to Caribbean creoles. [1][2]

### Why does the 500-versus-six figure matter?

Because it measures the ability to respond to incidents rather than to build models. Citing the World Development Report 2026, the UN says high-income countries have 500 incident response teams and low-income countries six — so a risk that travels across borders meets very unequal defences. [3]

### Does India have obligations under this?

No. It is a non-binding call. India's own rules are a separate matter, including the [IT Rules amendments on synthetic media](/article/india-deepfake-rules-what-platforms-must-do/).
