## The bottom line

**India did not pass a deepfake law. It amended the rules that already govern online platforms, and that is why the duties bite so broadly.**

On 10 February 2026 the government amended the Information Technology (Intermediary Guidelines and Digital Media Ethics Code) Rules, 2021 through notification G.S.R. 120(E), to address what the rules call **"synthetically generated information"** — "harms arising from synthetically generated information (SGI), including deepfakes and AI-generated content", in MeitY's description. [1][2]

Two things make this consequential. The obligations fall on whoever provides the tool *and* whoever hosts the result. And the penalty for getting it wrong is not a fine — it is losing the safe harbour that lets a platform operate without being liable for what its users post.

## What counts as a deepfake, legally

Rule 2(1)(wa) defines synthetically generated information as information "artificially or algorithmically created, generated, modified or altered using a computer resource, in a manner that such information appears to be real, authentic or true and depicts or portrays any individual or event in a manner that is, or is likely to be perceived as indistinguishable from a natural person or real-world event". [1]

Read the opening words carefully: the definition applies to **"audio, visual or audio-visual information"**. [1] AI-generated *text* falls outside it.

That matters because government summaries are looser, describing the threat as including "synthetic audio, video and text". [2] The operative definition is what creates the duty, and it stops at audio and images.

### What the rules deliberately exclude

A proviso keeps ordinary software out of scope. Information is **not** synthetically generated where it arises from: [1]

- "routine or good-faith editing, formatting, enhancement, technical correction, colour adjustment, noise reduction, transcription, or compression that does not materially alter, distort, or misrepresent the substance, context, or meaning" of the underlying content;
- "routine or good-faith creation, preparation, formatting, presentation or design of documents, presentations, portable document format (PDF) files, educational or training materials, research outputs" — provided no "false document or false electronic record" results;
- use of computer resources "solely for improving accessibility, clarity, quality, translation, description, searchability, or discoverability, without generating, altering, or manipulating any material part" of the underlying content.

Most summaries skip this, and it is what decides whether an ordinary product is caught. Noise reduction, auto-captioning and translation are out. A convincing fake of a real person is in.

## The four clocks

The amendment's most concrete effect is on deadlines. All four were shortened. The previous values are recorded in the consolidated rules' own amendment footnotes, and MeitY's July 2026 announcement lists the same reductions. [1][4]

| Trigger | Deadline | Previously |
|---|---|---|
| Actual knowledge via court order or a reasoned written government intimation | **3 hours** | 36 hours |
| Complaint about content exposing private areas, nudity, sexual acts, or impersonation "including artificially morphed images" | **2 hours** | 24 hours |
| Removal requests under rule 3(1)(b), with stated exceptions | **36 hours** | 72 hours |
| Any other grievance | Acknowledge 24 hours, resolve in **7 days** | 15 days |

**The three-hour clock** is the one the government leads on. Where an intermediary gets "actual knowledge" that it hosts unlawful information, it must "remove or disable access to such information within three hours". Actual knowledge "shall arise only" from a court order or a reasoned written intimation — nothing else starts the clock. [1]

**The two-hour clock** is the one an individual is most likely to use. On a complaint from the person affected or someone acting for them, about content exposing their private areas, showing nudity or sexual conduct, or impersonating them "including artificially morphed images", the intermediary must "take all reasonable and practicable measures to remove or disable access to such content". [1]

Note that wording: an obligation of effort within two hours, not a guarantee of removal. But a platform that cannot even act on such a complaint inside two hours is not meeting the rule.

### Who can start the three-hour clock

Not just anyone. An intimation must come from an officer authorised in writing, "not below the rank of Joint Secretary", or for the police one "not below the rank of Deputy Inspector General". It must specify "the legal basis and statutory provision invoked, the nature of the unlawful act, and the specific" URL or identifier — a sweeping demand does not qualify. And every intimation is reviewed by an officer "not below the rank of the Secretary" of that government, monthly, "to ensure that such intimations are necessary, proportionate, and consistent with" the statutory basis. [1]

So the window shortened from 36 hours to three, with a rank floor, a specificity requirement and a proportionality review alongside. Whether three hours leaves a platform room to assess a notice on its merits is the open question: that review is monthly, and after the fact.

## If you build a tool that generates synthetic media

Rule 3(3) sets a two-tier duty on any intermediary offering a computer resource that can create or alter synthetic media. [1]

**Tier one: block.** Deploy "reasonable and appropriate technical measures, including automated tools or other suitable mechanisms" to stop users generating or sharing synthetic content that breaks any law — the rules name the IT Act, the Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita 2023, the POCSO Act 2012 and the Explosive Substances Act 1908 — and specifically content that: [1]

1. contains child sexual exploitative and abuse material, non-consensual intimate imagery, or is "obscene, pornographic, paedophilic, invasive of another person's privacy, including bodily privacy, vulgar, indecent or sexually explicit";
2. results in "any false document or false electronic record";
3. relates to "the preparation, development or procurement of explosive material, arms or ammunition";
4. "falsely depicts or portrays a natural person or real-world event by misrepresenting, in a manner that is likely to deceive, such person's identity, voice, conduct, action, statement, or such event as having occurred".

**Tier two: label everything else.** Permissible output must be "prominently labelled in a manner that ensures prominent visibility in the visual display that is easily noticeable and adequately perceivable", or for audio "through a prominently prefixed audio disclosure" — plus "permanent metadata or other appropriate technical provenance mechanisms, to the extent technically feasible, including a unique identifier, to identify the computer resource" used to create it. [1]

And you may not let anyone strip it: the intermediary "shall not enable the modification, suppression or removal of the label, permanent metadata, including the unique identifier". [1]

The "to the extent technically feasible" qualifier applies to the metadata, not the visible label. On how fragile provenance metadata is in practice, see our piece on [the race to watermark everything AI makes](/article/quiet-race-watermark-everything-ai-makes/).

## If you run a platform above the threshold

Rule 4(1A), also inserted on 10 February 2026, applies to **significant social media intermediaries** — those with 50 lakh or more registered users in India. [1][2] Before any information is displayed, uploaded or published, an SSMI must: [1]

1. "require users to declare whether such information is synthetically generated information";
2. "deploy appropriate technical measures, including automated tools or other suitable mechanisms, to verify the accuracy of such declaration, having regard to the nature, format, and source of such information";
3. where the declaration or verification confirms it is synthetic, ensure it is "clearly and prominently displayed with an appropriate label or notice".

Step two is the demanding one. The rule does not accept a user's word: an SSMI's responsibility "shall extend to taking reasonable and proportionate technical measures to verify the correctness of user declarations and to ensure that no synthetically generated information is published without such declaration or label". [1]

That is a legal requirement to detect synthetic media at upload — and detection is not a solved problem, as our explainer on [whether AI detectors can prove who wrote something](/article/can-ai-detectors-prove-who-wrote-it/) sets out. "Reasonable and proportionate" leaves the standard to enforcement.

## The safe-harbour mechanics

This is where the real pressure sits, and the amendment moves in both directions.

**It protects platforms that act.** A new clause 1B clarifies that removing or disabling access in compliance with these rules — including "by deploying reasonable and appropriate technical measures, including automated tools" — "shall not amount to a violation of the conditions specified under clauses (a) or (b) of sub-section (2) of section 79 of the Act". [1] Taking content down does not itself cost you safe harbour.

**It punishes platforms that do not.** Where an SSMI "knowingly permitted, promoted, or failed to act upon such synthetically generated information in contravention of these rules, such intermediary shall be deemed to have failed to exercise due diligence". [1] Failing due diligence is what removes section 79 protection.

A further clause provides that references to information used to commit an unlawful act "shall be construed to include synthetically generated information". [1] Existing offences now read onto synthetic media without new ones being written.

## What a person can actually do

Alongside the rules, MeitY lists Grievance Appellate Committees, the SAHYOG portal for "automated, centralized removal notices", the National Cyber Crime Reporting Portal and helpline 1930. [2]

The practical route: complain to the platform's Grievance Officer, then appeal. "Any person aggrieved by a decision of an intermediary's Grievance Officer may appeal to the Grievance Appellate Committee within 30 days of the receipt of communication from the Grievance Officer." [3]

The appellate portal also gives a sense of the load. On 24 November 2026 it displayed **85,381 appeals received and 18,439 disposed**. [3] Roughly one in five — a useful counterweight to the two-hour headline, because a right that takes months to vindicate on appeal is a different thing from one honoured at the first step.

## What this means for…

**Indian startups building generative products.** Rule 3(3) applies to you, not only to large platforms, if your product outputs audio, images or video. The label and embedded identifier are build requirements, and you must prevent their removal. Read the proviso against your actual feature list.

**Compliance teams.** The two-hour clock needs staffing and routing, not policy. The upload-time verification duty is a detection problem the rule assumes you can solve.

**Everyone else.** Labels are now legally required in India, so a missing label will increasingly read as a claim of authenticity. That inference is weak — labels can be absent because a rule was broken — so do not treat an unlabelled video as verified.

## What we don't know yet

- **The exact commencement.** The amending notification is dated 10 February 2026, but the consolidated text we read carries no separate effective date for G.S.R. 120(E), so we do not assert one. [1]
- **How "technically feasible" will be judged** for permanent metadata, or what counts as "reasonable and proportionate" verification at upload. Both are the crux of compliance; neither is defined. [1]
- **Whether AI-generated text will be brought in.** Government descriptions mention text; the definition does not cover it. [1][2]
- **Enforcement to date.** We found no published count of actions taken specifically under the February 2026 synthetic-content provisions.

## FAQ

### What is a "deepfake" under Indian law?

The rules use the term "synthetically generated information": audio, visual or audio-visual information artificially or algorithmically created or altered so that it appears real and portrays a person or event in a way likely to be perceived as indistinguishable from the real thing. [1]

### Do the rules cover AI-generated text?

No. The definition is limited to audio, visual and audio-visual information, even though government summaries of the deepfake problem also mention text. [1][2]

### What is the two-hour rule?

On a complaint about content showing an individual's private areas, nudity or sexual conduct, or impersonating them "including artificially morphed images", an intermediary must take all reasonable and practicable measures to remove or disable access within two hours. It was 24 hours before the February 2026 amendment. [1]

### Does every AI tool have to add a label?

Any intermediary offering a resource that can create or alter synthetic media must label permissible output prominently and embed permanent metadata with a unique identifier where technically feasible. The proviso excludes routine editing, formatting, transcription, translation and accessibility work. [1]

### Which platforms count as "significant"?

Social media intermediaries with 50 lakh or more registered users in India. [2]

### Where do I complain if a platform does nothing?

To the platform's Grievance Officer first, then to the Grievance Appellate Committee within 30 days of its reply. [3]
