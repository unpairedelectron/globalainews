Title: EU Kids Act targets overly friendly AI chatbots | Rapporteur

![](https://www.rapporteur.com/content/uploads/2026/09/GettyImages-2270910069.jpg)EU Commissioner for Tech Sovereignty, Security and Democracy Henna Virkkunen and European Commission President Ursula von der Leyen \[Thierry Monasse/Getty Images\]

Commission President Ursula von der Leyen and Tech Commissioner Henna Virkkunen officially presented the EU Kids Act on Thursday, proposing bloc-wide rules to limit minors’ access to online services.

The law targets a broad range of platforms – from social media to AI chatbots, games and app stores – as _Euractiv_ [reported](https://www.rapporteur.com/news/gamers-will-have-to-prove-their-age-under-eu-kids-act/) earlier.

Under the approach, Virkkunen said AI chatbots and companions would no longer be able to act as “human friends” to underage users – curbing the risk of children becoming emotionally dependent on AI.

**‘Clear boundaries’**

Big Tech platforms were not designed with “children’s well-being and development in mind”, said von der Leyen, arguing ”clear boundaries” are needed to safeguard kids from tech giants’ commercial imperative to capture attention.

The Commission is taking a differentiated approach to applying restrictions on teens’ use of tech, reflecting how each age has its own sensitivities and needs for protection, she said.

The final draft of the law [sets](https://www.rapporteur.com/news/eu-kids-act-will-reset-big-tech-vows-von-der-leyen/) a minimum age of 15 for children to set up accounts on in-scope services themselves.

But – from age 13 onwards, and with parental permission – kids would be allowed to have “mini accounts”. These restricted accounts, which must be set up by a parent or guardian, would limit social features and restrict usage to a maximum of one hour per day.

“Parents cannot compete or keep up against algorithms, and they should not have to,” von der Leyen argued, though she also suggested there’s a need to better communicate the digital world’s risks and benefits to parents and teachers.

**Safety by design**

Addressing concerns that the EU push for online age gates could risk downgrading content safety standards, von der Leyen said: “An age limit does not mean letting tech companies off the hook for the content of the platform.”

Platforms must prove their services are safe by design for minors under 18, she stressed.

Other safety-by-design requirements for minor accounts include a ban on addictive features or the use of profiling-based content recommender systems, among other measures.

The law will make age checks mandatory for many platforms. Social media and video-sharing platforms must perform age verification on new accounts and estimate the age of existing users, per the [press release](https://ec.europa.eu/commission/presscorner/detail/en/ip_26_1890).

Von der Leyen said platforms could rely on privacy-safe EU-certified tools, such as the Commission’s “zero-knowledge proof” age verification app which only tells a service “one single thing”: whether a user is old enough for access or not.

A senior EU official also said that other in-scope services – such as AI companions, chatbots and online games – will be obliged to use age assurance methods.

The law will set out the difference between age verification and assurance, the same official same – with the former demanding a precise age and the latter inferring age based on techniques such as behaviour analysis or credit card information.
