## 1. India’s data-centre pipeline is not operational capacity

**Slug:** `india-data-centre-h1-2026`  
**Topic:** Infrastructure  
**Proposed dek:** Cushman & Wakefield counted 1,789 MW of operational capacity across seven Indian markets at H1 2026, alongside 3,860 MW of projects expected through 2030. Those are different stages, not one live supply figure.

### A pipeline is not a running facility

India’s data-centre market ended the first half of 2026 with 1,789 megawatts of operational capacity across seven markets, according to Cushman & Wakefield. The same update identified 3,860 MW of additional capacity expected through 2030. The second figure is a development pipeline, not a count of buildings already operating. It includes 509 MW under construction and 3,351 MW at the planning stage. [@cw-india-h1-2026]

That distinction is the main news in the numbers. The published pipeline is more than twice the operating base, but most of it was still at the planning stage when the report was released. A project can move from an announcement to construction, commissioning and operation only after land, power, financing, equipment, network connections and customers line up. A pipeline total measures intended future supply; it does not tell a tenant what is available today. [@cw-india-h1-2026]

The update says the seven markets added 178 MW of capacity during the first half of the year. That gives the 1,789 MW figure a specific reporting period and geographic scope: it is the operational total for the report’s top seven markets at H1 2026, not a count of every facility under every possible national definition. Economic Times and The Tribune both reported the update and attributed the figures to Cushman & Wakefield; the Tribune item is an ANI wire report, while the Economic Times report is by its own bureau. Neither is an independent measurement of the market. [@cw-india-h1-2026] [@et-cw-india-dc-2026] [@tribune-cw-india-dc-2026]

### The stages answer different questions

“Operational” is the most useful stage for readers asking how much capacity is in service. “Under construction” indicates projects that have progressed beyond planning, but it still does not mean their capacity can be purchased or scheduled. “Planning” is further from delivery. Combining those stages into a single total can make a future build-out look like present supply.

The next report should be read as a stage transition, not as a new block to add to the existing total. The 509 MW under construction and 3,351 MW in planning are components of the 3,860 MW pipeline; they should not be added to the 1,789 MW operational base as if every project were deliverable at once. A useful update would show how much moved from planning to construction, from construction to operation, and how much was removed or revised, using the same market coverage and reporting date. That is a request for a consistent series, not a prediction that any specific project will progress. [@cw-india-h1-2026]

The report’s 3,860 MW pipeline can be stated without obscuring the stages: 509 MW under construction plus 3,351 MW in planning. Those components add to the reported total, but they do not establish how much is financed, when each site will be energized, or how quickly customers can obtain capacity. The source does not provide a conversion from facility megawatts to a number of AI accelerators, model-training hours or inference requests. [@cw-india-h1-2026]

That last omission matters because data-centre capacity and AI compute access are related but not interchangeable. A data centre may serve cloud, enterprise, storage, communications or other workloads. Even a facility designed for high-density computing still needs suitable power delivery, cooling, networking and equipment before a specific AI workload can run. The H1 update is a property-market snapshot; it is not an inventory of GPUs offered to researchers or startups.

### Geography does not erase the definition problem

The report covers seven named markets and describes new capacity across them. That makes it useful for tracking the geography of investment, but the total should not automatically be compared with another publisher’s national estimate. A comparison needs the same date, market coverage, capacity definition and treatment of projects under construction. Without those controls, a difference may reflect scope rather than a sudden change in the physical stock.

The public conversation often places a data-centre pipeline beside national AI-compute programmes. That can be useful context, but it is not a direct reconciliation. One metric describes facilities by development stage and location; another might count accelerator units onboarded, capacity offered to users, or projects approved for subsidised access. Each answers a different question. A headline that collapses them risks turning planned real estate into supposedly available compute.

### What to watch next

The next useful update is not only a larger pipeline total. Readers need to know which projects reached construction or operation, what definition of capacity the report uses, how much is committed to actual workloads, and whether the named markets’ figures can be compared with government totals. A consistent time series would show whether the pipeline is converting into live facilities rather than simply growing as a list of proposals.

For now, the defensible reading is narrow: Cushman & Wakefield reported 1,789 MW operational in its seven-market universe at H1 2026 and a further 3,860 MW expected through 2030, most of it still planned. The figures signal an ambitious build-out, but they do not show that 3,860 MW is running, available, or dedicated to AI. [@cw-india-h1-2026]

**Evidence note:** The MW totals are attributed to Cushman & Wakefield. The two news reports corroborate the announcement and attribution, not the underlying measurement independently. Do not convert facility MW to GPUs or AI workload capacity.

## 2. IEA reports data-centre electricity use rose 17% in 2025

**Slug:** `iea-data-centre-electricity-2025-brief`  
**Topic:** Infrastructure  
**Proposed dek:** The IEA estimates that data-centre electricity consumption rose about 70 terawatt-hours in 2025. That is a sharp sector increase, but it sits inside a much larger rise in total electricity demand—and it is not an AI-only measure.

### A fast-growing slice of a larger increase

Data centres used about 17% more electricity in 2025 than in 2024, according to the International Energy Agency’s Global Energy Review 2026. In absolute terms, the increase was approximately 70 terawatt-hours. The IEA places that figure beside a roughly 800 TWh increase in total global electricity demand over the same period. [@iea-ger-2026-electricity-demand]

The comparison changes the scale of the headline. Seventy terawatt-hours is a large amount of electricity and a meaningful rise for grids, utilities and developers planning new supply. But it is not the whole increase in global demand. Using the IEA’s rounded figures, 70 divided by 800 is about 9%. That is an approximate share of the year-on-year increase, not data centres’ share of total electricity use. The calculation inherits the rounding and scope of the reported estimates. [@iea-ger-2026-electricity-demand]

The IEA’s accompanying announcement says demand from AI-focused data centres grew even faster than demand from data centres overall, while global electricity demand rose about 3%. It does not make the overall 17% data-centre increase equivalent to 17% growth in AI electricity use. The two figures have different scopes: one is for the data-centre sector, the other describes a faster-growing subset without assigning that subset the full sector total. [@iea-ger-2026-news]

### “Data centre” is not another name for “AI”

Data centres run a mix of services: cloud applications, business software, storage, networking, search, streaming and AI workloads, among others. Their power use can rise because customers add more computing, because existing systems run more intensively, or because the mix of equipment and services changes. A sector-wide electricity figure captures the facility category, not a single application.

That is why the IEA’s wording about AI-focused sites matters. It signals faster growth within that subset, but the reported 70 TWh increase is for data centres as a whole. The published summary does not provide a separate absolute number for AI-focused electricity consumption in the quoted comparison. Assigning the sector total to AI would overstate what the source measures.

Any narrower estimate should make its allocation rule visible. Readers need to know whether it counts facility electricity or only IT equipment, whether it covers AI-focused sites or AI workloads inside mixed facilities, and how shared cooling and other infrastructure are assigned. It should also state the period and whether the value is metered, estimated or modeled. Those fields would not create a new AI total; they would show which denominator a source uses and make two claims comparable. If the allocation method is missing, keep the description at the level the evidence supports: data-centre electricity, not electricity used by AI alone.

If no such allocation method is published, preserve the IEA’s sector-level label and avoid attaching the 17% growth rate to a particular company, country or model family.

### The denominator includes much more than computing

Bloomberg’s coverage of the report highlights that industry, household appliances and commercial buildings remained the larger contributors to the increase in electricity demand, even as electric vehicles and data centres grew quickly. The framing is useful because a high growth rate from a relatively small base can coexist with larger absolute increases elsewhere. [@bloomberg-iea-ger-2026]

The distinction is important for policy. A grid planner cannot size generation, transmission or demand-response around a growth percentage alone. The relevant questions include where the load appears, when it peaks, how much is flexible, what equipment it serves, and how quickly infrastructure can connect. The IEA’s global figure describes a broad trend; it does not settle local capacity or reliability questions.

The same caution applies to carbon claims. Electricity consumption is not automatically a direct measure of emissions. Emissions depend on the generation mix and the time and location of consumption, alongside other system conditions. A global TWh figure can show how much electricity a sector uses, but it cannot by itself show the emissions attributable to AI or the carbon effect of a particular facility.

### What the number can support

The IEA result supports three bounded statements. Data-centre electricity use rose substantially in 2025; the IEA estimates the increase at roughly 70 TWh; and AI-focused data-centre use rose faster than the sector average. It also supports a contextual comparison: total global electricity demand rose by about 800 TWh, according to the report. [@iea-ger-2026-electricity-demand] [@iea-ger-2026-news]

It does not show that AI caused all 70 TWh of growth, that the increase occurred evenly across countries, or that one model or company drove it. Nor does it forecast future demand. Those questions require disaggregated evidence about sites, workloads, utilisation and power sources.

For readers assessing the environmental cost of AI, the most useful next step is therefore to ask for workload-level and location-specific data: what share of a facility’s electricity serves AI, when that load runs, and what source supplies it. The global IEA estimate establishes that data centres are growing quickly as electricity consumers. It also shows why a sector headline should not be mistaken for a precise measure of AI’s individual footprint. [@bloomberg-iea-ger-2026]

**Evidence note:** The 17% and 70 TWh figures are IEA estimates for data centres overall. AI-focused sites grew faster, but the cited summary does not assign them the full 70 TWh increase. The approximately 9% comparison is arithmetic from rounded IEA values, not an IEA-published percentage.

## 3. EU KIDS Act proposal would set new rules for AI companions

**Slug:** `eu-kids-act-ai-companions-proposal`  
**Topic:** Policy  
**Proposed dek:** The European Commission’s September proposal would add child-specific safeguards for digital services, including AI companions. It is a legislative proposal, not a rule that providers must already follow.

### A proposal, not an enacted obligation

The European Commission adopted its EU KIDS Act proposal on 17 September 2026. The accompanying policy page describes it as a proposal intended to strengthen protection for minors online. The draft would set requirements for services used by children, including provisions relevant to AI systems and conversational companions. It is not yet an enacted EU regulation. [@eu-kids-act-policy-page-2026] [@eu-kids-act-proposal-2026]

That legal status is the first fact to keep clear. A Commission proposal begins a legislative process; it does not become binding law simply because the Commission has published it. The Council’s related document is dated 18 September, while the Commission’s proposal and policy page use 17 September. These records describe the same policy initiative from different institutional steps, so the dates should not be merged into one unexplained “passed” date. [@eu-kids-act-proposal-2026] [@eu-kids-act-council-document-2026]

Euractiv’s coverage describes one particularly visible proposal: AI chatbots and companions should not present themselves as human friends to underage users, and providers would be required to use age-assurance measures. The proposal’s purpose is to address risks associated with minors’ interaction with digital services, including the possibility that a conversational system could encourage emotional dependence. These are proposed safeguards and policy objectives, not evidence that every chatbot currently behaves this way. [@euractiv-kids-act-2026]

### The measure is broader than a chatbot rule

The Commission presents the KIDS Act as a broader child-safety framework for online services. Its policy page describes proposed restrictions on account creation for children under 15 on specified social-networking and video-sharing services, alongside requirements intended to make services safer for minors. The proposal also contemplates age-appropriate access rather than treating all minors as one group. The exact obligations depend on the final legislative text and any amendments during negotiation. [@eu-kids-act-policy-page-2026] [@eu-kids-act-proposal-2026]

For AI companions, the policy question is not simply whether a system can hold a conversation. A rule against presenting a chatbot as a human friend would concern how the service frames the relationship and what protections it provides to young users. Age assurance concerns how a service determines which safeguards apply. The proposal’s approach would therefore intersect product design, account controls, disclosure, and service governance.

The distinction also matters for implementation. Age-assurance systems can impose privacy and access costs if they collect more information than necessary. A final law would need to be read for its precise duties, permitted methods, and responsibility boundaries. The Commission’s proposal creates the starting text; the final obligations can change through legislative negotiation and technical guidance. [@eu-kids-act-proposal-2026]

### Existing rules still matter

The proposed act is not the only EU instrument relevant to minors and AI. The Commission says the proposal would complement existing digital-services and AI legislation. That means providers should not interpret the KIDS Act as a replacement for existing duties, nor treat the proposal as proof that all child-related AI risks are already covered by one consolidated rule.

For an AI companion provider, practical preparation can begin without pretending that the proposal is law. Teams can identify which users are minors, review how the product describes its social role, test whether it encourages dependence, and examine what age-assurance measures would be proportionate. Those steps are risk management, not a substitute for legal advice or a claim that a particular draft clause will survive unchanged.

An interim product review can make those questions auditable. For each relevant feature, record which age groups can reach it, what the interface says about whether the interaction is with AI, what behavior the feature invites, and what reporting or escalation path is available. Separately note the age-assurance approach being considered and what user information it would require. Mark every item as current behavior, a proposed safeguard or an unresolved legal question; do not label a draft clause as a settled duty. Revisit the register when the Parliament and Council publish positions and again against the adopted wording, noting which clause or product decision changed. This is planning for uncertainty, not a compliance determination.

### What happens next

The Commission’s policy page says the proposal will proceed through the EU legislative process and invites feedback through 26 November 2026. The text therefore has a long path between publication and any final, enforceable obligations. Readers should watch for the Parliament’s and Council’s positions, amendments to the proposal, and later implementation guidance. [@eu-kids-act-policy-page-2026]

The feedback date is a consultation milestone, not an adoption or compliance date. A comment window can shape the public record without settling which provisions survive negotiation or when any final rule applies. News coverage should keep those milestones separate and update the description only when a later institutional document changes the proposal’s status or text. [@eu-kids-act-policy-page-2026]

The immediate development is the proposal itself: Brussels has placed child safety for AI companions and other digital services into a formal legislative process. The practical stakes are real, but the legal status is still provisional. Until the text is adopted and its application dates arrive, the careful description is “proposed,” not “required.” [@eu-kids-act-proposal-2026] [@euractiv-kids-act-2026]

**Evidence note:** The Commission proposal and policy page are primary sources. Euractiv corroborates the news and describes the companion provisions. Do not state that the proposal is enacted or infer final compliance duties from the draft.

## 4. OpenAI adds GPT-6 Sol and Luna below its flagship tier

**Slug:** `openai-gpt6-sol-luna-release`  
**Topic:** Models  
**Proposed dek:** OpenAI’s 22 September release adds two lower-priced GPT-6 models for work and coding. The company’s launch benchmarks are not independent tests, and the models’ initial product availability was not universal.

### Two models, different prices

OpenAI announced GPT-6 Sol and GPT-6 Luna on 22 September 2026, positioning them as less expensive ways to use capabilities introduced with GPT-6 Astra. The company’s published API prices are $2 per million input tokens and $10 per million output tokens for Sol, and $0.10 per million input tokens and $0.50 per million output tokens for Luna. OpenAI says these prices are 50% lower than the corresponding GPT-5.6 promotional pricing; that comparison is specific to the earlier promotion, not a claim that every workflow will cost half as much. [@openai-gpt6-sol-luna-2026]

The product split is as significant as the price list. OpenAI describes Sol as suited to harder professional and coding work, while Luna is the faster, lower-cost tier. Those are the company’s intended-use descriptions. A team choosing between them still needs to test its own tasks, because a lower token price does not guarantee a lower total bill if a model uses more tokens, needs more retries, or fails to complete the job. [@openai-gpt6-sol-luna-2026]

Reuters reported the launch on the same date and attributed capability and efficiency claims to OpenAI. The release is therefore independently reported as a product announcement, but that does not make the company’s benchmark results independent performance measurements. OpenAI’s own release provides the detailed methods and caveats for its evaluations. [@reuters-gpt6-sol-luna-2026] [@openai-gpt6-sol-luna-2026]

### Benchmarks need their conditions

OpenAI presents comparisons across professional workflows, factuality, coding and computer use. For example, it reports Sol’s performance on AutomationBench and Agents’ Last Exam, and explains the task sets, reasoning-effort settings and per-task costs. The results are useful as a record of what the company tested, but they are not a universal ranking. The benchmarks use particular versions, tools and effort settings; competitor results may come from published reports rather than a jointly controlled run. [@openai-gpt6-sol-luna-2026]

The company also says its factuality evaluation uses de-identified conversations in which users had flagged model errors. OpenAI warns that those conversations are not representative of ordinary usage and that its scores are not controlled for response length. That makes the caveat part of the result, not a footnote to ignore. A test built from known error-inducing examples can help compare models on that task, but it does not directly state the probability of an error in a typical conversation. [@openai-gpt6-sol-luna-2026]

The launch page’s benchmark discussion includes additional methodological boundaries. OpenAI says GPT evaluations were performed in its research environment or through its API, which can differ from production ChatGPT because system prompts and available tools vary. It also notes that some Claude Fable 5 comparisons use results from Fable 5 where Fable 5.1 scores were unavailable. Those qualifications make the numbers informative but not perfectly like-for-like. [@openai-gpt6-sol-luna-2026]

### Availability is staged

At launch, OpenAI said Sol and Luna were available in ChatGPT Work and Codex for Plus, Pro, Business, Enterprise and Edu users, with Luna available to Free and Go users in the desktop app. The company said they were not yet available in Chat, while API access used the model names `gpt-6-sol` and `gpt-6-luna`; it also described a gradual rollout through ChatGPT Work and Codex. A launch announcement is a dated availability snapshot, not a guarantee that every account or product surface received the models at the same moment. [@openai-gpt6-sol-luna-2026]

For developers, prompt caching is another part of the cost story. OpenAI says cached input reads receive a 90% discount and describes controls for monitoring and optimizing cache use. The practical savings depend on whether an application reuses prefixes and how often those requests hit the cache. The feature can lower costs in a well-matched workflow; it does not reduce every token charge by 90%. [@openai-gpt6-sol-luna-2026]

A budget should therefore keep uncached input, cached input and generated output as separate quantities. A workflow with a stable repeated prefix may have a different bill from one whose input changes on every call, while long generated responses remain exposed to the output rate. Compare those quantities on representative tasks and report the observed cache-hit rate rather than applying the maximum discount to every request. This is a calculation method based on the published rate structure, not a claim that either model will be cheaper for a particular customer. [@openai-gpt6-sol-luna-2026]

### What buyers should take from the release

The launch establishes a product and pricing change: OpenAI added two GPT-6 tiers with sharply different API prices and a broader set of initial access points. It also provides the company’s own evaluation record and methods. It does not establish that Sol or Luna is the best choice for every workload, that headline benchmark rankings predict an organization’s tasks, or that the prices alone determine total cost.

Teams evaluating the models should record the task, prompt, tools, reasoning effort, output length, retry rate and completion quality. They should compare the resulting cost per successfully completed task—not just the advertised price per million tokens. OpenAI’s release offers hypotheses to test; the organization’s own workload supplies the decision. [@reuters-gpt6-sol-luna-2026] [@openai-gpt6-sol-luna-2026]

**Evidence note:** Release dates, API prices and availability come from OpenAI’s page and are corroborated as an announcement by Reuters. Benchmark scores and efficiency claims are OpenAI-reported. No claim here treats them as an independent cross-vendor evaluation.

## 5. Google’s Gemini 3.8 Audio card maps four voice-model variants

**Slug:** `gemini-38-audio-model-cards`  
**Topic:** Models  
**Proposed dek:** Google DeepMind’s 15 September card lists two Live models and two text-to-speech models, with different availability and stated limits. The card is a product record, not an independent test of voice quality.

### One family, four distinct variants

Google DeepMind published its Gemini 3.8 Audio model card on 15 September 2026. It lists Gemini 3.8 Live, Gemini 3.8 Live Extended Thinking, Gemini 3.8 Flash TTS and Gemini 3.8 Flash-Lite TTS. Grouping all four under “audio” is useful for finding the release, but the card describes different functions: real-time interactive models sit alongside text-to-speech variants. [@google-gemini-38-audio-card]

The Live models are described as handling continuous streams of audio, video and text to produce spoken responses. The card also lists product and platform surfaces where different variants are available, including the Gemini API, AI Studio, the Gemini app and several Google products. Availability varies by model and surface, so the family-level list should not be read as every variant being available everywhere. [@google-gemini-38-audio-card]

The card also separates input and output capacity by function. Live and Live Extended Thinking list audio, image, video and text input up to 128K tokens, and audio and text output up to 64K; the Live Avatar configuration lists video with audio and text output up to 24K. The two TTS variants accept text input up to 8K and return audio, with a 64K output limit. These are interface specifications, not evidence of intelligibility or response speed. [@google-gemini-38-audio-card]

That split can guide a prototype before any quality comparison. A one-way narration or read-aloud feature maps to a text-to-speech path; an interaction that must accept streamed audio, video or text and answer in speech maps to the Live family. If an avatar is required, its modality and the card’s short-session limitation need to be considered separately. A fair test should compare variants on equivalent jobs rather than treating “audio” as one common task. First establish that the interface fits; then measure quality and failure modes for the product’s actual language and acoustic conditions.

TechTarget reported the introduction of Gemini 3.8 Live and Live Extended Thinking on 16 September, describing the tools as a way to build voice agents that can reason and make calls while a conversation continues. That supports the release and intended product direction. The capability descriptions still originate with Google; the article does not supply a controlled independent benchmark of latency, language quality or task success. [@techtarget-gemini-38-live-2026]

### The card is more useful than a launch adjective

Model cards help readers move past labels such as “natural,” “expressive” or “real time.” They identify the exact variant, describe the supported inputs and outputs, list where the model is offered, and disclose limitations. For a developer, that information can establish whether a service exposes audio input, video input, generated speech, or a combination. It can also prevent a mistake such as selecting a text-to-speech model when the application needs a two-way conversational session.

The card’s availability section is especially important because product names can mask different deployment paths. A model may be reachable through a developer API but not through a consumer app, or be exposed in one enterprise tool before another. The card lists availability by model family and surface; it is not a service-level guarantee of latency, regional access, uptime or a particular quota. Those details belong in current product documentation and terms. [@google-gemini-38-audio-card]

### Stated limitations still shape a prototype

Google’s card warns of familiar generative-model limitations, including hallucinations, occasional slowness and timeouts. It also gives a January 2025 knowledge cutoff for the card’s described models. For systems expected to answer current questions, that reinforces the need for a current retrieval source and a visible failure path rather than relying on a voice model’s built-in knowledge alone. [@google-gemini-38-audio-card]

The card says Live Avatar versions can generate a video response with head movement and synchronized speech, but only for a few minutes of continuous interaction. That limitation changes what a product team can responsibly promise. A short interactive demonstration is not evidence of a long-running avatar session, and the behavior of one variant should not be generalized to every model in the family. [@google-gemini-38-audio-card]

Google’s 2 October monthly roundup describes the September audio releases as Gemini 3.8 Live and distinct text-to-speech variants, rather than treating “Gemini 3.8 Audio” as one undifferentiated product. It is a company recap, not an independent evaluation. [@google-ai-september-roundup-2026]

### What the card establishes—and what it does not

The card establishes that Google documented four audio-related variants, their intended modality roles, listed availability surfaces and several stated limitations. It lets a reader distinguish real-time conversational models from speech-generation variants before building a prototype. [@google-gemini-38-audio-card]

It does not establish that all four are equally capable, that a spoken response is always low-latency, or that the models outperform another provider’s system. Those are empirical questions requiring comparable tasks, measurement conditions and independent reporting. The card is still valuable because it tells builders what Google says it released and where the boundaries of its description lie.

For teams testing a voice workflow, a practical evaluation should measure interruption handling, end-to-end response delay, transcription error, tool-call success, recovery after timeouts, and performance across the languages and acoustic conditions the product actually serves. The model card provides the map of the family; the production task supplies the test. [@techtarget-gemini-38-live-2026] [@google-gemini-38-audio-card]

**Evidence note:** The model variants, availability list and limitations come from Google DeepMind’s first-party card. TechTarget independently reports the Live release but does not validate the card’s performance claims.
